## Why a fully explained case is worth a full entry

Mexico 2004 is the cleanest demonstration in the database of a specific failure mode: **an infrared
sensor detecting real objects that are nowhere near where the crew believes they are.**

The crew saw eleven heat sources apparently pacing and surrounding their aircraft. That perception
was sincere and their instrument was working correctly. The heat sources were real.

They were also stationary, on the surface, roughly 200 km away — gas flares on oil platforms. The
apparent motion and encirclement came from the aircraft’s own turn.

## The pattern this belongs to

This is the Aguadilla lesson and the GoFast lesson in a third form. In every case:

- the sensor worked,
- the operators were competent and honest,
- and the interpretation failed because **range was unknown and assumed.**

Three independent incidents, three countries, three decades of technology, one identical error. That
recurrence is itself a finding, and it is why this database treats sensor geometry as a first-class
subject rather than a footnote.

## Institutional note

Mexico’s Defence Ministry released the footage promptly and publicly. When the prosaic explanation
emerged, that release is what made checking possible. Openness with raw material is what allows
cases to be solved — and it is the argument for releasing the Nimitz data too.
