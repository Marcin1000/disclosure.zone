## Why this is probably the strongest historical military case

Tehran has one advantage over every other case of the era, Nimitz included: **an operational
document written almost immediately after the event and distributed at the highest level of the
American intelligence structure.**

This is not an interview given thirty years later. It is a report that exists because an institution
judged the event serious enough to report — before anyone knew it would become famous.

## The structure of the event

The sequence is long and layered:

1. Four independent civilian reports to the command centre.
2. Personal observation by the operations commander, who orders the intercept.
3. First F-4: loss of instruments and communications on approach, restored on turning away.
4. Second F-4: radar contact at about 27 NM, closure rate about 150 knots, target holding distance.
5. A second object separating from the first and moving toward the fighter.
6. An attempt to launch an AIM-9 — weapons panel failure and loss of communications.

No single mistake generates that structure.

## Where the limit lies

The entire sequence comes from **one document.** There are no airborne radar recordings, no F-4
system logs, no communications recording. The DIA report is an excellent source for **what the
participants reported** — it is not an independent measurement of what physically occurred.

That still places Tehran high. But the difference between “an institution reported this” and "this
was measured" has to stay visible.

## One element worth separate study

A double system failure in two different aircraft, correlated in time with range to the target and
reversible on withdrawal. If there is one physical effect in the entire UAP corpus worth
re-examining by engineers, it is this one.
