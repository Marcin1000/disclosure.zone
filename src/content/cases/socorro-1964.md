## Why this is the best classic close encounter

Three things put Socorro above hundreds of similar accounts:

1. **A professional witness reporting immediately.** Zamora was an officer on duty and reported the
   event by radio while it was in progress. There is no thirty-year memory gap here.
2. **A physical trace.** Indentations in the ground, burns, damaged vegetation. The site was secured
   and photographed within hours.
3. **A serious investigation.** Blue Book and the state police worked the case, and an FBI agent who
   happened to be in Socorro was at the site the same evening; the FBI office in Albuquerque itself,
   by its own teletype, conducted no investigation and kept liaison with the military. No explanation
   was found.

## What the FBI recorded

The FBI's file 62-HQ-83894 holds what its Albuquerque office sent to Washington: an urgent teletype of
25 April 1964 (Section 9, pp. 226–228), an airtel of 28 April (pp. 232–236) and a report of 8 May
with Zamora's own account (Serial 438).

The report of 8 May says that Special Agent D. Arthur Byrnes, Jr., was at the State Police office in
Socorro on other business; that "At approximately 5:45 to 5:50 P. M." the sheriff's radio operator
came in with Zamora's call about an object which "landed and has taken off"; and that Byrnes finished
his work "at approximately 6:00 P. M." and went to the site (Serial 438, p. 2). There he noted:

> "These depressions appeared regular in shape, approximately sixteen by six inches rectangular. Each
> depression seemed to have been made by an object going into the earth at an angle from a center
> line. Each depression was approximately two inches deep and pushed some earth to the far side."

> "There were three circular marks in the earth which were smooth, approximately four inches in
> diameter and penetrated in the sandy earth approximately one-eighth of an inch as if a jar lid had
> gently been pushed into the sand." (p. 3)

Three burned patches of grass lay inside the four depressions and one outside them. The teletype of
25 April puts the depressions "IN RECTANGULAR TYPE PATTERN AVERAGING ABOUT TWELVE FEET APART" (pp.
226, 228), and two hand-drawn sketches in the file give the distances between the marks (Serial 438,
pp. 38, 40). Of Zamora the report says he was "known intimately for approximately five years" to the
agent and "was noted to be perfectly sober and somewhat agitated over his experience"; the airtel of
28 April, recording what the state police sergeant and the undersheriff found when they answered his
calls, puts it this way (p. 235):

> "Officer ZAMORA, a well regarded and capable officer, was noted to be perfectly sober and thoroughly
> frightened."

The airtel adds that "there appeared to be no "blast" effects", and that a copy of Zamora's statement
and "a small sample of charred material taken at the scene" were kept at the Albuquerque office. The
teletype notes that the military was conducting an operation "KNOWN AS CLOUD GAP" and that it was "NOT
KNOWN IF ABOVE INCIDENT RELATES TO CLOUD GAP" (p. 228).

## Where the weakness lies

One observer of the event itself. No radar. No footage. The ground traces prove that **something
physical exerted pressure and heat at that spot** — they do not prove what it was.

## Stating the hoax hypothesis fairly

The student-prank hypothesis has circulated for decades and deserves serious treatment. Its problem
is that no material confirmation was ever produced: no apparatus, no credible admission, no
reconstruction reproducing the traces and the sound. It remains possible but unproven — exactly like
every other hypothesis in this case.
