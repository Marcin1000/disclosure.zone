## A necessary correction to the popular version

RB-47 is presented in the literature as a case of “three independent radars”. **That is untrue, and
it matters.**

The RB-47H's onboard equipment was an ELINT / direction-finding suite — **a passive receiver of
other emitters**, not a radar detecting reflections from objects. That means:

- the system gave **bearing** and signal characteristics,
- the system **did not measure range**,
- and therefore the onboard data cannot reconstruct a trajectory or a speed.

Without range there is no speed. That sentence is the spine of this database, and nowhere is it
sharper than here.

## What remains strong

Despite the correction, the case stays in the first class, because we have **three different kinds
of channel**: a passive electromagnetic signal, a visual sighting, and ground reports. Operator
Frank McClure described a signal near 2.8 GHz whose bearing shifted relative to the aircraft in a
way that did not fit a fixed ground emitter. Pilot Lewis Chase independently described an intense
blue-white light.

That is exactly the kind of correlation a single mistake does not readily generate.

## What was missing, and will stay missing

No complete, time-synchronised set exists: ELINT tapes plus ground station logs plus aircraft
position minute by minute. The reconstructed chronology rests heavily on interviews conducted **a
decade after the event.** That sharply limits what can be claimed today.
