## The case this database needed most

Every wave in this corpus is reconstructed. Levelland, the 1896 airships, the Belgian wave: all of
them are read backwards through documents written afterwards, by people arguing about what had
already happened.

This one was documented **while it was happening.** Reports, agency responses, flight restrictions,
congressional briefings and the eventual assessment all exist with dates on them. There is no gap
between the event and the record for a legend to grow in.

That makes it the most useful control case here.

## What actually happened

From mid-November 2024, residents of New Jersey began reporting large drones flying at night, often
in formation, often near sensitive sites. Reports spread to neighbouring states. Within weeks the
story had reached national television, state legislators were demanding answers, and the FAA had
issued temporary flight restrictions over specific locations.

The federal response was unusually explicit for this subject. Four agencies published joint
statements. They said the reports were a mixture of lawful drones of several kinds and, in a large
share of cases, ordinary manned aircraft and stars misjudged for drones. Of more than 5,000 tips,
about 100 produced leads worth following. Nothing anomalous was identified.

## Why so many people saw so much

Two mechanisms account for most of it, and both are demonstrated elsewhere in this toolkit.

**Range is not perceptible at night.** A light in a dark sky carries no distance information. An
aircraft at 3,000 metres and a quadcopter at 60 metres can subtend the same angle and look identical.
Once a viewer has been told to expect drones, the aircraft becomes a drone.

**Attention creates reports, and reports create attention.** The count of sightings rose with the
coverage, not with anything in the sky. This is the same feedback loop visible in the 1896 airship
wave, a century before drones existed, and in the Italian wave of 1978.

## What this case does not prove

It does not show that nothing unauthorised flew over New Jersey. Roughly 100 leads were followed and
their individual outcomes are not all public. “We identified nothing anomalous” is a statement about
what was found, not a guarantee about what was there.

Nor does it retire the Colorado drone swarms of 2019, which remain unresolved in this database. The
two look alike and ended differently, and that difference is the point: the same shape of event can
resolve or not depending on what was recorded, not on what was seen.

## What would strengthen the record further

Release of the per-lead dispositions rather than the aggregate. The agencies published a conclusion
and a pair of totals; the working that produced them stayed inside. For a case whose whole value is
that it was documented in the open, that is the one gap worth closing.
