## Why a photograph with no witness is one of the stronger artefacts here

Almost every famous UFO photograph has the same defect: a person decided to take it. Someone chose
the moment, the framing and the exposure, and then chose whether to keep the result. Every one of
those choices is a place where a picture can be made rather than taken.

This frame has none of them. A cartographic camera bolted into a survey aircraft fired on a timer
every 13 seconds. Nobody aimed it at anything. Nobody on board reported seeing an object. The disc
was found later, by someone looking at a strip of survey film for an entirely different reason: the
mapping of a valley that would be flooded for the Arenal hydroelectric scheme.

**That is what provenance means here.** Not that the object is unexplained, but that the mechanism
which produced the image had no interest in the object at all.

## What the frame supports

The image is a high-resolution black-and-white negative from a precision instrument, held by a state
agency, with a flight record establishing altitude, time and interval. Reviewers who have examined it
report a defined edge, illumination consistent with the rest of the frame, and no sign of montage.

The 13-second interval matters more than it appears. If the disc were a defect in the film or the
optics, it would have no reason to appear on exactly one exposure. Its absence from both neighbours
means that whatever produced it was present for a period shorter than the interval, or moved out of
frame within it.

## What the frame does not support

It does not establish size, distance or altitude. A photograph taken from a single station cannot.
The disc overlaps the shoreline, which places some geometric constraints on where it can be, but a
constraint is not a measurement. Anyone quoting a diameter for this object is quoting an assumption
about range with the assumption removed.

It also has **no second channel.** There is no radar track, no second camera, no witness, no ground
report. On the scale used here that costs the case heavily, and it should. One instrument recording
one anomaly is exactly the situation in which an instrument fault is hardest to rule out.

## What would settle it

A high-resolution scan of the original negative, published with the adjacent frames and the flight
log, would let anyone repeat the analysis instead of taking it on trust. Photogrammetry against the
mapped shoreline, which is the whole point of a survey frame, could narrow the object's position
along the line of sight far better than any published estimate has.

The material to do this exists and is in one place. That is more than most cases in this database
can say.
